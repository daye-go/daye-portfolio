Customizable Karaoke POS System

Background

Vpos is a customizable POS system whose screens and features can adapt to different store types. This project defined the karaoke version, where staff manage room time, drink orders, reservations, sales, and schedules from the same device.

The existing product was based on a restaurant flow: select menu items, send orders, and take payment. Karaoke venues work differently. They sell time first, room status changes continuously, and drink orders are added on top of each room's session. The restaurant-based POS did not have a time selection or time management function.

The largest operational issue was onboarding. Karaoke venues often rely on part-time staff, so a new employee needs to understand the POS quickly from the first shift. Reducing training time and lowering the entry barrier became the main design problem.

Approach

One design criterion held everything together: someone seeing the screen for the first time should understand what to do next. The screen needed to be direct enough for a first-day employee, while still flexible enough for different karaoke venue layouts.

That criterion set the order of work. First, make the daily repetitions — room time management and drink orders — easy to enter without training. Then bring reservation management into the POS. Finally, make sales and staff schedules visible from the same system.

Feature 01
Time management & drink orders

Room time and drink orders were designed as one connected flow

Time selection and remaining time
I designed the time selection the restaurant POS never had. Tap a room, choose the session length, and the remaining time counts down live once it starts. Extensions and early closes are handled on the same screen.

The layout is organized by room, mirroring the physical floor. Which rooms are occupied and which are free reads at a glance, without parsing a list.

Drink orders
Orders are added with the room already selected. Time and orders stay bound to the same room, so the session fee and the drinks total are combined automatically at checkout.

"A first-day employee should be able to open a room, start the clock, add drinks, and take payment without needing a walkthrough."

The frequent actions sit one tap away on the first screen: open a room, select time, extend time, add an order, and take payment. Fewer actions buried in menus means the next step can be inferred rather than taught.

Feature 02
Reservations, sales & schedules

Reservations, sales, and schedules moved into the POS employees already use

Reservation management
Reservations arrived by phone and lived on paper or in messenger threads. Details leaked whenever the person on duty changed, and double bookings happened. I brought reservations into the POS — room, time, and party size recorded together, surfacing on the room itself when the slot comes up.

Reservations and current occupancy share one screen, so checking whether a room can be taken right now doesn't require opening anything else.

Sales and schedule access
Staff can check sales figures and their own shift schedule from the POS. Previously this meant asking the owner or looking it up through a separate channel.

This was the largest piece of the project. It moved the information staff check every day, but which previously lived outside the POS, into the device they already use during each shift.

Learning

Changing the business type wasn't a matter of swapping screens. A restaurant POS rests on the premise that you sell menu items; karaoke rests on the premise that you sell time. When the premise changes, the information architecture has to be rebuilt. That's why adding a time picker was never going to be enough.

Cutting training time didn't have to mean cutting features. Putting frequent actions on the first screen and letting the layout communicate sequence kept the feature set intact while keeping first-time users from getting lost.

What's left
The project closed without quantitative measurement. Given another run, I'd compare training time, time-to-first-completed-payment for a new hire, and double-booking incidents before and after release. All three were already felt as problems in the venue, so they wouldn't have been hard to instrument.

Change the business and you change what's being sold; change what's being sold and you change how the screen is structured.
