They Left Before the Algorithm Knew Them. We Fixed It — D30 Retention 51% → 79%.

Background

StyleShare was losing its 20s users — and fast. Churn rate in that cohort exceeded 50%, well above other age groups. The gut reaction was a content problem, but user interviews told a different story.

20s users weren't leaving because the product was bad. They were leaving because it never felt like theirs. Across sessions, we kept hearing the same thing: "이 서비스는 10대 느낌이라 내가 찾는 옷이 없어요." The platform had built its identity around teen fashion, and 20s users — despite being a critical growth cohort for revenue — were getting a feed that had nothing to do with them.

This was declared a company-wide problem. Our growth squad took ownership.

My Role

I was the only designer on the growth squad. Every experiment we ran — every screen, flow, and A/B variant — went through me. Beyond execution, I drove the ideation process itself: running Sprints and Crazy 8s with the PM, data analyst, and engineers to generate hypotheses, then owning the design as DRI from concept to ship.

Every experiment followed the same discipline: hypothesis first, design second, data always.

Diagnosis
An Activation Problem, Not a Content Problem

20s users were leaving in the first few sessions — before personalization ever kicked in.

Our data analyst mapped drop-off by cohort. The pattern was clear: 20s users were churning in the first few sessions, not after extended use. This was an Activation and Cold Start problem — not a content depth problem.

New users arrived with no taste profile. The algorithm defaulted to the platform's dominant signal: teen fashion. 20s users saw irrelevant content immediately, concluded the product wasn't for them, and left before personalization ever had a chance to correct itself.

The feedback loop:

No taste data → Teen-skewed feed → 20s disengage → Algorithm never corrects → More churn. A self-reinforcing trap that no amount of new content could break — unless we fixed the cold start.

Before designing anything, I translated the diagnosis into three named hypotheses — each mapping to a known recommender failure mode, so every later experiment had something specific to disprove.

1. Collaborative Filtering — cold start with no onboarding
Recommendations leaned on app metadata rather than on the user. New users never saw "content made for me" on the first screen, and we identified this as the primary driver of early churn.

2. Popularity Bias — a gap between expectation and product
With no interaction history, recommendations misfired and new users never engaged with the core components. Closing this gap was the path to an aha moment.

3. Data Collection — biased recommendations surfacing off-taste items
The app held rich content assets, but which asset to show a new user first had never been validated. With no agreed priority between them, it had to be tested from scratch.

Experiment 01
Onboarding Profile — Fixing the Cold Start

We looked outside fashion to design the onboarding. That was the insight that made it work.

The highest-impact initiative I led was designing StyleShare's first real onboarding system. The reference frame was deliberately cross-category.

We looked at Tinder for interaction design — swipe-style selection that makes preference input feel like play, not a form. We looked at Pinterest for data strategy — collecting enough initial signals to seed the recommendation algorithm before a user ever reaches the home feed.

"The goal was dual: give users a genuinely fun, low-friction way to express fashion taste — and collect first-party preference data to cold-start personalization from session one."

Five steps, three signal types

The algorithm needed three different kinds of signal. Rather than ask for them in one form, I split them across five steps — each step carrying its own activation, with motion loading screens turning the wait into anticipation.

StepActivation
Pick 5 styles you like (hashtags)Hashtag follow
"Building your recommended styles…"Motion loading — wait becomes anticipation
Swipe like / skip on style cardsLike — preference input as play
Follow the brands you likeBrand follow
"Generating your list…"Motion loading — personalization made visible

We ran multiple A/B test variants — different card formats, selection counts, visual treatments, and progression logic — to find the version that maximized completion rate and downstream retention. This wasn't a single launch; it was an iterated series of experiments.

The home screen that data unlocked

Before — the layout made it hard to find brands users actually wanted, so follow rates stayed low. Worse, it surfaced brands with no relation to their interests, and users read that as "this app isn't for me."

After — two changes. Brands followed during onboarding moved to the top of the feed, so the screen said "this is your style" on sight, with product imagery exposed directly to shorten the path to purchase. And the styles a user liked were clustered by machine learning, replacing endless scrolling with a feed that opened on what they came for.

Experiment 02
AHA Moment — The Weekly Trend Report

"What do I wear today? What is everyone else wearing?" — we answered it weekly.

Every week we surfaced the users and styles with the sharpest growth in likes and followers. High-quality content raises how the service itself is perceived, and perception is what pulls people back. The experiment ran on three mechanics.

FOMO — the weekly trend summary
Showing this week's most-loved content scratched the "what do I wear this week?" itch and used the fear of falling behind a trend to drive participation. Users could swipe through the week's styles at a glance, browse by category, and read current trends off the most-used hashtags.

Viral loop — status as a reward
Being selected for the Weekly Newsletter was framed as an honor. Selected users uploaded more, which enriched the content pool, which pulled in more traffic and participation — a loop that compounds instead of a campaign that ends.

Participation — interaction over consumption
Moving past one-way content delivery, we pushed comments, likes, and follows between users so people spent longer inside the platform. Best reviews were shown alongside the products they featured, giving reviews a direct path to purchase.

What the experiment surfaced

1. Newsletter selection worked as a genuine behavioral trigger — the desire for recognition turned out to be a strong enough motivator to drive uploads on its own.
2. Content quality maps directly onto service perception. Simply showing selected users' styles was enough to plant the idea that this is a platform worth being on.
3. Community and personalization reinforce rather than compete. Selected users felt both belonging and individual recognition at once, and that combination produced the strongest engagement we measured.

Experiment 03
Let's Share — Turning Loyal Users into Marketers

The cheapest acquisition channel we had was the users already here.

Users posted with campaign hashtags and promoted StyleShare events on their own. The structure came from the Referral stage of AARRR — grow the customer base organically while cutting paid marketing spend. We ran it as three chained hypotheses.

HypothesisGoal
Rewards will increase the number of users postingActivation through rewards
Hashtag-based posting will produce organic referral and viralityReferral traffic via hashtags
Referral traffic will lift brand awareness and bring new usersBrand awareness and new-user acquisition

Hashtag activation
Each campaign could be entered per hashtag, with entry steps kept short and clearly explained. Showing who had already joined turned participation into social proof, and competing to become a featured user drove uploads further.

Personalization, refined
Participation hashtags were fed back in as a personalization signal — related posts and products were surfaced to drive conversion, so every campaign entry made the following recommendations sharper.

Cross-platform referral
Rewards also encouraged posting to Instagram, reaching people who had never used StyleShare. High-quality posts carried brand perception outward at no media cost.

Awareness
A home-screen popup caught users who didn't know a campaign was running, with a CTA leading straight into the entry flow.

Also shipped
Alert, Recommendation & Loyalty Systems

Three more workstreams. Each targeting a different part of the retention funnel.

Alert & Recommendation Experiments
We ran a series of mid-funnel engagement experiments: cart-based similar item recommendations surfaced relevant alternatives to saved or abandoned items, and personalized push notifications were tested across timing, copy, and trigger conditions to re-engage dormant users. Each variant was grounded in a clear hypothesis, run as an A/B test, and evaluated against conversion metrics.

Home Feed Algorithm Redesign
Beyond the onboarding cold start fix, we iterated on the feed algorithm itself — defining which signals to surface and how to weight fresh user preferences against platform-wide trending content, ensuring the personalization loop strengthened with every session.

Points & Tier System Renewal
To pull churned users back, we redesigned the loyalty and reward system — building a structural habit loop rather than relying on one-off campaigns. The renewal contributed to improved Resurrection Rate among previously inactive users.

What I Learned: Design in a Growth Context Is Hypothesis Design

Running experiments as the sole designer taught me that design in a growth context is hypothesis design as much as visual design. The question isn't "what looks good" — it's "what assumption are we testing, and how do we isolate the variable?"

The Tinder/Pinterest reference was a deliberate UX strategy call. Both products had solved onboarding for preference collection better than any fashion e-commerce product I'd seen. Borrowing the interaction model from outside the category was the key insight that made the onboarding system work.

The result: Day 30 retention went from 51% to 79%, and purchase conversion from 48% to 75%. Not through a redesign. Through the right experiment, run at the right moment in the user journey, with enough iterations to find the version that actually worked.
